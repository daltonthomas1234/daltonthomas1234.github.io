Texas federal judge rules marketing texts are not "calls" under the Do Not Call lawsuit provision (Siringi v. Crust Pizza Co.)

On November 6, 2026, a federal judge in the Southern District of Texas dismissed the federal claims in a proposed class action over marketing texts from a pizza restaurant, ruling that texts are not "telephone calls" under the part of the federal telemarketing law that lets consumers bring their own Do Not Call lawsuits. It is the first ruling we have found from a federal district judge in Texas reaching that result, and another judge in the same district has ruled the other way.

CaseSiringi v. Crust Pizza Co.
CourtSouthern District of Texas (S.D. Tex., 5th Circuit)
Docket / citationNo. 4:25-cv-04266, ECF No. 40
JudgeDrew B. Tipton, U.S. District Judge
DecidedNovember 6, 2026
Type of rulingRuling on a contested motion to dismiss, after review of a magistrate judge's recommendation and the plaintiff's objections (granted as to the federal claims)
HoldingText messages are not "telephone calls" under 47 U.S.C. § 227(c)(5), so the plaintiff's federal Do Not Call claim over texts was dismissed. The judge agreed with the Seventh Circuit's Steidinger decision and found it more persuasive than an earlier Southern District of Texas ruling that went the other way.

What happened in this case

Cosby Siringi sued Crust Pizza Co. in federal court in Texas as a proposed class action, meaning he asked to represent other people in a similar situation. His lawsuit is about text messages. He brought claims under two parts of the Telephone Consumer Protection Act (TCPA), a 1991 federal law about telemarketing, and under a Texas law, Texas Business and Commerce Code § 305.053, which lets people sue over communications that violate the TCPA or its rules. These are allegations; the court did not decide whether they are true.

The restaurant asked the court to dismiss the case. A magistrate judge, Dena H. Palermo, reviewed the motion first and on October 14, 2026 recommended granting it. Siringi filed objections. The district judge assigned to the case, Drew B. Tipton, then decided the motion himself.

Key terms

  • Do Not Call list (National Do Not Call Registry): a free federal list where people can register their phone number to opt out of most telemarketing.
  • 47 U.S.C. § 227(c)(5): the part of the TCPA that lets a person who received "more than one telephone call within any 12-month period" from the same company, in violation of the telemarketing rules, bring their own lawsuit.
  • Motion to dismiss: a request asking the judge to end a case early because, even if everything the plaintiff says is true, the law does not give him a claim.
  • Magistrate judge's recommendation: a magistrate judge can review a motion and recommend a result. The district judge then reviews it again, looking fresh at any part a party objects to, and makes the actual decision.

What the court decided

Judge Tipton agreed with the recommendation that the Do Not Call claim fails, after reviewing that question fresh because Siringi objected. His main reasons, in plain terms:

  • The lawsuit provision, § 227(c)(5), covers only someone who received more than one "telephone call." Nearby parts of the law instead use the broader term "telephone solicitation," which Congress defined to include "the initiation of a telephone call or message." The judge wrote that he "finds this difference in language significant and agrees with the Seventh Circuit" that the lawsuit provision is narrower.
  • An earlier ruling in the same district, Alvarez v. Fiesta Nissan (January 2026), had held that texts are calls. Judge Tipton wrote: "Although Alvarez reached the contrary conclusion, the Court finds Steidinger's treatment of the statutory text and structure more persuasive."
  • Siringi pointed to Supreme Court and Fifth Circuit cases that described texts as calls. The judge said those cases dealt with a different part of the law, § 227(b), which covers autodialed and prerecorded calls.
  • He also said later changes Congress made to a different section, and the Federal Communications Commission's rules on text solicitations, do not change what § 227(c)(5) allows people to sue over.

Siringi's separate claim under § 227(b) was also dismissed, but for different reasons: the judge said the complaint did not plausibly show an autodialer was used and that Siringi had not defended that claim. The judge made clear that this dismissal was "not on a determination that text messages categorically fall outside Section 227(b)."

The judge did not accept the recommendation on the Texas-law claim. He said that a failed federal claim does not automatically mean the Texas claim fails, because the Texas law also covers violations of the FCC's rules. He held off on deciding that claim and asked both sides to file short briefs within 14 days on whether the federal court should keep hearing it. He also noted that his ruling on § 227(c)(5) "does not determine the validity of an otherwise applicable FCC regulation."

What it could mean in the Southern District of Texas

With this order, the map on tcpamap.com now shows the Southern District of Texas in yellow (judges disagree). The district had been shown green (text treated as a call) based on the earlier Alvarez ruling. This new order goes the other way, so judges in the district have now reached opposite results on the same question.

This is not a finding about whether anyone did anything wrong; it is a ruling about what the federal statute allows people to sue over. One trial judge's ruling does not bind other judges in the district, so the outcome may depend on which judge hears a case. The Fifth Circuit, which covers Texas, Louisiana and Mississippi, has not decided this question, and the ruling could be reviewed on appeal. The Texas-law claim in this case is still pending.

The bigger picture

On July 14, 2026, the Seventh Circuit decided Steidinger v. Blackstone Medical Services, holding that § 227(c)(5) does not let people sue over texts. It is the only federal appeals court decision on this exact question so far, and it binds the federal courts in Illinois, Indiana and Wisconsin. Judge Tipton agreed with its reasoning.

Since then, district courts have split. Several outside the Seventh Circuit have followed Steidinger, while courts in the Ninth Circuit have declined to. Until this order, the Texas federal rulings we had found treated texts as calls. Some districts are now split by judge: in the Southern District of Florida, different judges have gone opposite ways, and with this order the Southern District of Texas has rulings going both ways too.

The question opened up partly because of the Supreme Court's 2025 decision in McLaughlin Chiropractic Associates v. McKesson Corp., which said courts in private lawsuits do not have to follow the Federal Communications Commission's reading of the TCPA, which had long treated texts as calls. Courts now decide the meaning themselves.

As of November 13, 2026, with this order included, the map on tcpamap.com shows 18 districts where a text was not treated as a call (red), 17 where it was (green), 2 where judges disagree (yellow) and 57 with no verified decision identified (grey), out of 94.
