Text treated as a callD. IdahoDecided November 16, 2026Posted November 19, 2026On the blog page

Idaho federal judge rules marketing texts can count as "calls" under the Do Not Call lawsuit provision (Vandersloot v. ATYG)

On November 16, 2026, a federal judge in Idaho refused to dismiss a lawsuit over more than 130 calls and texts offering business funding, ruling that a text message can be a "telephone call" under the part of the federal telemarketing law that lets consumers bring their own Do Not Call lawsuits. The judge expressly declined to follow the Seventh Circuit's contrary Steidinger decision. It is the first ruling on this question we have found in the District of Idaho.

CaseVandersloot v. ATYG, LLC
CourtDistrict of Idaho (D. Idaho, 9th Circuit)
Docket / citationNo. 4:26-cv-00275-DCN, ECF No. 26
JudgeDavid C. Nye, U.S. District Judge
DecidedNovember 16, 2026
Type of rulingRuling on a contested motion to dismiss (denied)
HoldingA text message may count as a "telephone call" under 47 U.S.C. § 227(c)(5), so Do Not Call claims based on marketing texts can go forward. The court also let the plaintiff's autodialer claim and his request for increased damages proceed.

What happened in this case

Matthew Vandersloot, who lives in Idaho, sued ATYG, LLC, a New York company. He says he put two phone numbers on the National Do Not Call Registry in January 2016, and that between June 2021 and June 2025 the company contacted him by text and phone call a total of 137 times from various numbers, using names he says are connected to ATYG, such as Steady Capital Solutions. He says he asked to be put on the company's internal do-not-call list 59 times before the contacts stopped. These are allegations; the court did not decide whether they are true.

The case started in Idaho state court and was moved to federal court. ATYG asked the federal court to dismiss it on several grounds, including that text messages are not "telephone calls" under the Telephone Consumer Protection Act (TCPA), a 1991 federal law about telemarketing.

Key terms

  • Do Not Call list (National Do Not Call Registry): a free federal list where people can register their phone number to opt out of most telemarketing.
  • 47 U.S.C. § 227(c)(5): the part of the TCPA that lets a person who received "more than one telephone call within any 12-month period" from the same company, in violation of the telemarketing rules, bring their own lawsuit.
  • Motion to dismiss: a request asking the judge to end a case early because, even if everything the plaintiff says is true, the law does not give him a claim.
  • Autodialer (ATDS): an "automatic telephone dialing system." A separate part of the TCPA, § 227(b), restricts calls made with this kind of equipment.

What the court decided

Judge David C. Nye denied the motion on every ground.

  • Texts can be calls. The judge noted that the Ninth Circuit, the appeals court over Idaho, has not directly decided this question under § 227(c)(5), but that its decisions under another part of the TCPA treating texts as calls "provide relevant guidance." He agreed with earlier California federal rulings, pointed out that the TCPA defines a telephone solicitation as "the initiation of a telephone call or message," and noted that the FCC's rules now say the Do Not Call protections apply to telemarketing texts to cell phones. He concluded that "a text message may constitute a 'telephone call' for purposes of § 227(c)(5)."
  • Steidinger rejected. The company relied heavily on the Seventh Circuit's Steidinger v. Blackstone Medical Services decision. The judge wrote that the court "respectfully declines to follow Steidinger and the district court decisions adopting its reasoning." He also noted that after the Supreme Court's Loper Bright decision he must use his own judgment about what the statute means, giving the FCC's view weight only to the extent it is persuasive.
  • Who sent the messages. The judge found the complaint gave enough detail connecting the calls and texts to ATYG for the case to continue. Whether the evidence proves that is for later.
  • Autodialer claim. The judge said repeated or automated messages alone do not prove an autodialer was used, but found that the 24 texts the plaintiff identified, with the details he gave about them, were enough to let that claim proceed to the evidence-gathering stage.
  • The judge also rejected the arguments that the complaint was confusingly written, that the messages were not telephone solicitations, and that the request for increased damages for knowing or willful violations should be thrown out.

What it could mean in the District of Idaho

With this order, the map on tcpamap.com shows the District of Idaho in green (text treated as a call). Before this ruling we had not found any decision on this question in the district, so it was shown grey.

This is not a finding that ATYG did anything wrong; the case now continues, and the company can still contest the facts and raise defenses. One trial judge's ruling does not bind other judges in the district. The Ninth Circuit has not decided this exact question under § 227(c)(5), and the ruling could be reviewed on appeal.

The bigger picture

On July 14, 2026, the Seventh Circuit decided Steidinger v. Blackstone Medical Services, holding that § 227(c)(5) does not let people sue over texts. It is the only federal appeals court decision on this exact question so far, and it binds the federal courts in Illinois, Indiana and Wisconsin. Every district court in the Ninth Circuit that we have found ruling on the question since then, now including this one, has declined to follow it.

Elsewhere, courts are divided. Some districts are split between judges, such as the Southern District of Florida and the Southern District of Texas. In Texas, on the same day as this order, a Northern District of Texas judge held in Starling v. What If Holdings that texts are not calls, while a Western District of Texas judge held in Waterman v. The General Automobile Insurance Services that they are.

The question opened up partly because of the Supreme Court's 2025 decision in McLaughlin Chiropractic Associates v. McKesson Corp., which said courts in private lawsuits do not have to follow the Federal Communications Commission's reading of the TCPA, which had long treated texts as calls. Courts now decide the meaning themselves.

As of November 19, 2026, with this order and the November 16 Northern District of Texas ruling included, the map on tcpamap.com shows 19 districts where a text was not treated as a call (red), 17 where it was (green), 2 where judges disagree (yellow) and 56 with no verified decision identified (grey), out of 94.
