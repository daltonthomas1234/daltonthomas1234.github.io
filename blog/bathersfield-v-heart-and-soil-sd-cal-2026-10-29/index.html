Text treated as a callS.D. Cal.Decided October 29, 2026Posted October 31, 2026On the blog page

San Diego federal judge rules early-morning marketing texts can be "calls" under the telemarketing law (Bathersfield v. Heart & Soil Supplements)

On October 29, 2026, a federal judge in San Diego refused to throw out a proposed class action over marketing texts allegedly sent before 8 a.m., ruling that texts count as "telephone calls" under the part of the federal telemarketing law that lets consumers bring their own lawsuits.

CaseBathersfield v. Heart & Soil Supplements, LLC
CourtSouthern District of California (S.D. Cal., 9th Circuit)
Docket / citationNo. 3:26-cv-01124-GPC-SBC, ECF No. 31
JudgeGonzalo P. Curiel, U.S. District Judge
DecidedOctober 29, 2026
Type of rulingRuling on a contested motion for judgment on the pleadings (denied)
HoldingText messages count as "telephone calls" under 47 U.S.C. § 227(c)(5), following Ninth Circuit decisions on texts as calls. The court declined to follow the Seventh Circuit's Steidinger decision.

What happened in this case

Charles Bathersfield says Heart & Soil Supplements, a supplement company, sent him marketing text messages at 6:11 a.m. on or about November 28, 2025, and at 5:14 a.m. on or about January 15, 2026, his local time. Federal telemarketing rules bar telephone solicitations to homes before 8 a.m. and after 9 p.m. He says he never authorized texts during those hours.

He sued under the Telephone Consumer Protection Act (TCPA), a 1991 federal law about telemarketing, as a proposed class action for people who received more than one such early or late marketing text from the company in a year. These are his allegations; the court has not decided whether they are true.

Key terms

  • Quiet-hours rule: a federal telemarketing rule that bars telephone solicitations to residential subscribers before 8 a.m. or after 9 p.m. local time.
  • 47 U.S.C. § 227(c)(5): the part of the TCPA that lets a person who received "more than one telephone call within any 12-month period" from the same company, in violation of the telemarketing rules, bring their own lawsuit. The same provision covers Do Not Call list claims.
  • Motion for judgment on the pleadings: a request, made after both sides have filed their opening papers, asking the judge to end a claim because, even if the plaintiff's facts are true, the law does not give them a claim.

What the court decided

The company argued that texts are not "telephone calls" under § 227(c)(5), and urged the court to follow the Seventh Circuit's Steidinger decision. Judge Gonzalo P. Curiel denied the motion. His main reasons, in plain terms:

  • The Ninth Circuit, the appeals court over California, held in Satterfield (2009) that a text message is a "call" under a neighboring part of the TCPA, and reaffirmed that in Howard v. Republican National Committee (2026), saying the result would be the same without deferring to the Federal Communications Commission. The judge found that reasoning applies equally to "telephone call" in § 227(c)(5).
  • On Steidinger, he wrote: "Steidinger does not change that result." He said he was not persuaded the Ninth Circuit would adopt its reasoning, noting that both courts started from similar dictionary definitions of "call."

The company also argued that the plaintiff had agreed to receive the texts. The judge said that dispute cannot be settled at this early stage, because the court has to treat the company's contrary factual claims as denied. He also refused the company's request for an early ruling that a class could never be certified.

What it could mean in the Southern District of California

On our map the Southern District of California is green: courts there have treated texts as calls under § 227(c)(5). This ruling keeps that pattern and is the most recent contested decision we have found in the district.

The ruling does not mean the company did anything wrong. The case now moves forward, and the plaintiff still has to prove his allegations. One trial judge's ruling also does not bind other judges, and the question could be decided differently on appeal.

The bigger picture

On July 14, 2026, the Seventh Circuit decided Steidinger v. Blackstone Medical Services, holding that § 227(c)(5) does not let people sue over texts. It is the only federal appeals court decision on this exact question so far, and it binds the federal courts in Illinois, Indiana and Wisconsin.

Since then, courts have split. Several district courts outside the Seventh Circuit have followed Steidinger, including, on the same day as this ruling, a federal judge in the Middle District of Pennsylvania. District courts in the Ninth Circuit, like this one, have declined to. Some districts are split by judge: in the Southern District of Florida, different judges have gone opposite ways.

The question opened up partly because of the Supreme Court's 2025 decision in McLaughlin Chiropractic Associates v. McKesson Corp., which said courts in private lawsuits do not have to follow the Federal Communications Commission's reading of the TCPA. Courts now decide the meaning themselves.

When this post was written (October 31, 2026), the map on tcpamap.com showed 18 districts where a text was not treated as a call (red), 18 where it was (green), 1 where judges disagree (yellow) and 57 with no verified decision identified (grey), out of 94.
