Text not treated as a callM.D. Pa.Decided October 29, 2026Posted October 31, 2026On the blog page

Pennsylvania federal judge rules marketing texts are not "calls" under the Do Not Call lawsuit provision (Bronstin v. Happy Cabbage Analytics)

On October 29, 2026, a federal judge in Harrisburg dismissed a proposed class action over two cannabis marketing texts, ruling that texts are not "telephone calls" under the part of the federal telemarketing law that lets consumers bring their own Do Not Call lawsuits. It appears to be the first ruling of its kind from a federal trial court in the Third Circuit.

CaseBronstin v. Happy Cabbage Analytics, Inc.
CourtMiddle District of Pennsylvania (M.D. Pa., 3rd Circuit)
Docket / citationNo. 1:25-cv-01083, ECF No. 50, 2026 WL 3257600
JudgeJennifer P. Wilson, U.S. District Judge
DecidedOctober 29, 2026
Type of rulingRuling on a contested motion to dismiss (granted in part, denied in part)
HoldingText messages are not "telephone calls" under 47 U.S.C. § 227(c)(5), so receiving unwanted marketing texts does not support a lawsuit under that provision. The court followed the Seventh Circuit's Steidinger decision and dismissed the case with prejudice.

What happened in this case

Asher Bronstin says he put his personal cell phone number on the National Do Not Call Registry in February 2021. According to his complaint, he received a text on March 9, 2022, advertising cannabis products sold by a business called The Spot. He replied "STOP" and got a reply saying he would be unsubscribed. He says another cannabis marketing text arrived on March 17, 2023.

Both texts came from phone numbers registered to Happy Cabbage Analytics, a marketing company that runs text campaigns for cannabis businesses. Bronstin sued Happy Cabbage under the Telephone Consumer Protection Act (TCPA), a 1991 federal law about telemarketing, as a proposed class action, meaning he asked to represent other people who got similar texts. These are his allegations; the court did not decide whether they are true.

Key terms

  • Do Not Call list (National Do Not Call Registry): a free federal list where people can register their phone number to opt out of most telemarketing.
  • 47 U.S.C. § 227(c)(5): the part of the TCPA that lets a person who received "more than one telephone call within any 12-month period" from the same company, in violation of the telemarketing rules, bring their own lawsuit.
  • Motion to dismiss: a request asking the judge to end a case early. The company made two arguments: that Bronstin had no standing (the right to be in court at all), and that even if everything he says is true, the law does not give him a claim.

What the court decided

Judge Jennifer P. Wilson split the motion. She ruled that Bronstin did have standing. Happy Cabbage had argued that its client, The Spot, sent the messages, but the judge said that question goes to whether Happy Cabbage is responsible, which is decided later, not to whether Bronstin can be in court.

On the main question, she ruled for the company. She held that § 227(c)(5) "does not provide a cause of action for the receipt of unwanted text messages." Her main reasons, in plain terms:

  • She looked at what the words meant in 1991, when Congress passed the law. Using the same dictionary the Seventh Circuit used, she explained that a "telephone call" then "referred to communication through sound," and "Text messages do not typically produce sounds."
  • Other parts of the TCPA talk about a telephone "call or message," but the lawsuit provision mentions only calls. She read that difference as deliberate. She also noted that Congress updated a different section of the law in 2018 to cover texts and left this one alone.
  • She said earlier appeals court decisions treating texts as calls, including the Third Circuit's 2013 decision in Gager v. Dell Financial Services, dealt with a different section of the law and relied on the Federal Communications Commission's view, which courts no longer have to follow. On the many courts that went the other way, she wrote that "the mere number of decisions reaching that conclusion is not dispositive."
  • She acknowledged the concern that this reading leaves consumers exposed as technology changes, but said "Congress can amend this statutory provision to include text messages."

Because Bronstin's claim rested on two text messages, she dismissed the case with prejudice, meaning it cannot be refiled in an amended form in this court.

What it could mean in the Middle District of Pennsylvania

The Middle District of Pennsylvania is red on the map on tcpamap.com (text not treated as a call). That color rests on this order, which is the first contested decision we have found there, and it went against treating texts as calls.

It is not a finding about whether anyone did anything wrong; it is a ruling about what the statute allows people to sue over. One trial judge's ruling also does not bind other judges in the district, and the decision could be appealed to the Third Circuit, which covers Pennsylvania, New Jersey and Delaware and has not decided this question since the Supreme Court's 2025 McLaughlin decision. Other federal judges in Pennsylvania and New Jersey, including in the Eastern District of Pennsylvania and the District of New Jersey, have ruled the other way.

The bigger picture

On July 14, 2026, the Seventh Circuit decided Steidinger v. Blackstone Medical Services, holding that § 227(c)(5) does not let people sue over texts. It is the only federal appeals court decision on this exact question so far, and it binds the federal courts in Illinois, Indiana and Wisconsin. Judge Wilson relied heavily on it.

Since then, district courts have split. Several outside the Seventh Circuit have followed Steidinger, while courts in the Ninth Circuit and in Texas have declined to. Some districts are split by judge: in the Southern District of Florida, different judges have gone opposite ways.

The question opened up partly because of the Supreme Court's 2025 decision in McLaughlin Chiropractic Associates v. McKesson Corp., which said courts in private lawsuits do not have to follow the Federal Communications Commission's reading of the TCPA, which had long treated texts as calls. Courts now decide the meaning themselves.

When this post was written (October 31, 2026), the map on tcpamap.com showed 18 districts where a text was not treated as a call (red), 18 where it was (green), 1 where judges disagree (yellow) and 57 with no verified decision identified (grey), out of 94.
