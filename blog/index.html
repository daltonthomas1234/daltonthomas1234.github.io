Text treated as a callN.D. Cal.Decided October 22, 2026Posted October 26, 2026# · Permalink

Northern California federal judge rules early-morning marketing texts count as "calls" (Arguello v. R.E.S.A.)

On October 22, 2026, a federal judge in the Northern District of California refused to throw out a lawsuit over two marketing texts sent before 8 a.m., ruling that texts count as "telephone calls" under the part of the federal telemarketing law that lets consumers bring their own lawsuits.

CaseArguello v. R.E.S.A., Inc.
CourtNorthern District of California (N.D. Cal., 9th Circuit)
Docket / citationNo. 3:26-cv-05536-JSC, ECF No. 36, 2026 WL 3169713
JudgeJacqueline Scott Corley, U.S. District Judge
DecidedOctober 22, 2026
Type of rulingRuling on a contested motion to dismiss (denied, after oral argument)
HoldingText messages qualify as "telephone calls" under 47 U.S.C. § 227(c)(5). The court said it was bound by Ninth Circuit precedent and declined to follow the Seventh Circuit's Steidinger decision.
This district on the map todayText treated as a call See all 94 districts on the map

What happened in this case

Kevin Arguello says a real estate company, R.E.S.A., Inc., sent him two marketing text messages through one of its real estate sales agents. According to his complaint, the texts arrived on July 2, 2025, at 7:36 a.m. and on September 29, 2025, at 7:14 a.m., his local time.

He sued under the Telephone Consumer Protection Act (TCPA), a 1991 federal law about telemarketing. He relies on a federal rule that bars telephone sales pitches to homes before 8 a.m. or after 9 p.m. local time (often called the "quiet hours" rule). He filed the case as a proposed class action, meaning he is asking to represent other people who got similar texts. The court has not decided whether a class will be allowed.

Note that this case is about the quiet-hours rule, not the National Do Not Call Registry itself. Both rules sit in the same part of the law, Section 227(c), and the same question about texts applies to both.

Key terms

  • Do Not Call list (National Do Not Call Registry): a free federal list where people can register their phone number to opt out of most telemarketing.
  • 47 U.S.C. § 227(c)(5): the part of the TCPA that lets a person who received "more than one telephone call within any 12-month period" from the same company, in violation of the telemarketing rules, bring their own lawsuit.
  • Motion to dismiss: an early request asking the judge to end a case because, even if everything the plaintiff says is true, the law does not give them a claim. The judge does not decide who is telling the truth at this stage.

What the court decided

R.E.S.A. asked Judge Jacqueline Scott Corley to dismiss the case. Its main argument was that § 227(c)(5) covers only telephone calls, and a text is not a telephone call. It relied on Steidinger v. Blackstone Medical Services, a July 2026 decision of the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit that agreed with that view.

After hearing argument on October 22, 2026, the judge denied the motion the same day. She wrote that the court denied it "because text messages qualify as 'telephone calls' under 47 U.S.C. § 227(c)(5)."

Her reasons, in plain terms:

  • California federal courts must follow the Ninth Circuit, the appeals court above them. Two Ninth Circuit cases, Satterfield v. Simon & Schuster (2009) and Howard v. Republican National Committee (2026), held that a text message is a "call" under the TCPA. Following Steidinger instead "would require the Court to contravene binding Ninth Circuit precedent."
  • Those Ninth Circuit cases came up under a different section of the law (Section 227(b), about autodialers and prerecorded messages). The company argued that "call" means something different in Section 227(c). The judge disagreed, explaining that a word is presumed to mean the same thing throughout a law.
  • The ordinary meaning of "call" (an attempt to communicate by telephone) and the law's purpose of protecting privacy both point the same way.

The company also suggested the quiet-hours rule might not apply because the texts went to a cell phone rather than a home landline. The judge rejected that too. She pointed to the rule's own text, which applies it to texts sent to wireless numbers, and to Ninth Circuit precedent treating cell phones as presumptively residential.

Finally, the company asked, for the first time in its reply brief, for permission to take an immediate appeal. The court declined to consider that request because it was raised too late.

What it could mean in the Northern District of California

On our map the Northern District of California is green: courts there have treated texts as calls under § 227(c)(5). This ruling adds to an earlier one from a different judge in the same district, Wilson v. MEDVIDI (Judge Freeman, October 7, 2025).

The ruling does not mean the company did anything wrong. The case now moves forward, and the plaintiff still has to prove his allegations. One trial judge's ruling also does not bind other judges. But because the judge treated the question as settled by Ninth Circuit precedent, this ruling suggests texts will keep being treated as calls in this district unless the Ninth Circuit or the Supreme Court says otherwise.

The bigger picture

The national split started in earnest on July 14, 2026, when the Seventh Circuit decided Steidinger. It held that § 227(c)(5) does not let people sue over texts. It is the only federal appeals court decision on this exact question, and it binds the federal courts in Illinois, Indiana and Wisconsin.

Since then, the trend has split largely along regional lines:

  • Several district courts outside the Ninth Circuit have followed Steidinger, including courts in Colorado, northern Alabama, western Virginia, western Missouri, the middle district of North Carolina, and the middle and southern districts of Florida.
  • Every district court in the Ninth Circuit that has ruled on it has declined to follow Steidinger. Besides this ruling, that includes the Central District of California (Miller; Combs), the Western District of Washington (Hopkins), and the Eastern District of California (Lewis, decided the day before this one). Judge Corley wrote that the court "joins every other district court in the Ninth Circuit."
  • Some districts are split by judge. In the Southern District of Florida, one judge ruled in September 2026 that texts are not calls (Phillips v. GJD Stars), while another judge in the same district ruled in November 2025 that they are (the Office Depot cases).

This question has opened up partly because of the Supreme Court's 2025 decision in McLaughlin Chiropractic Associates v. McKesson Corp. It said courts in private lawsuits do not have to follow the Federal Communications Commission's reading of the TCPA, which had long treated texts as calls. Courts now decide the meaning themselves.

Eastern California federal court treats marketing texts as "calls" in its first ruling on the question (Lewis v. Tenth Hall)

On October 21, 2026, a federal court in the Eastern District of California refused to dismiss a Do Not Call lawsuit over marketing texts, ruling that texts count as calls under the federal telemarketing law. It is the first ruling on the question that we have found in this district.

CaseLewis v. Tenth Hall, Inc. (Mom N Pop's Realty LLC)
CourtEastern District of California (E.D. Cal., 9th Circuit)
Docket / citationNo. 2:26-cv-01810, 2026 WL 3152283
JudgeAllison Claire, U.S. Magistrate Judge (deciding the case on the parties' consent)
DecidedOctober 21, 2026
Type of rulingRuling on a contested motion to dismiss (denied)
HoldingThe TCPA applies to text messages, so texts are "calls" for a Do Not Call claim under 47 U.S.C. § 227(c)(5). The court called the Seventh Circuit's Steidinger decision non-binding and not persuasive, and refused to allow an immediate appeal.
This district on the map todayText treated as a call See all 94 districts on the map

What happened in this case

Edward Lewis says his cell phone number has been on the National Do Not Call Registry since December 14, 2004. He alleges that on or about March 21, 2026, and April 9, 2026, the defendant sent him marketing text messages about its property, goods or services. He says he had not done business with the company in the prior 18 months, had not asked about its products in the prior three months, and never gave permission for marketing texts.

He sued under the Telephone Consumer Protection Act (TCPA) as a proposed class action, meaning he is asking to represent other people on the Do Not Call list who got similar messages. No class has been approved.

Key terms

  • Do Not Call list (National Do Not Call Registry): a free federal list where people can register their phone number to opt out of most telemarketing.
  • 47 U.S.C. § 227(c)(5): the part of the TCPA that lets a person who received "more than one telephone call within any 12-month period" from the same company, in violation of the Do Not Call rules, bring their own lawsuit.
  • Motion to dismiss: an early request asking the judge to end a case because, even if everything the plaintiff says is true, the law does not give them a claim. The judge accepts the complaint's facts as true for this purpose only.
  • Magistrate judge on consent: both sides agreed to have a U.S. magistrate judge handle the whole case instead of a district judge. She acts in place of a district judge for this case.

What the court decided

The company made two arguments: that the TCPA does not apply to text messages, and that the complaint was too vague because it did not list the phone number that got the texts. Magistrate Judge Allison Claire rejected both and denied the motion.

On the texts question, she noted that the company's argument rested on the Seventh Circuit's Steidinger decision, which she called non-binding authority that "contradicts the rule regularly applied in the Ninth Circuit." She explained that "[t]he Ninth Circuit has held that a text message is a 'call' within the meaning of the TCPA," citing Satterfield v. Simon & Schuster (2009) and Howard v. Republican National Committee (2026).

She also agreed with another California ruling, Combs v. UG Franchise Operations (Central District of California, October 2, 2026). That court reasoned that although the two Ninth Circuit cases arose under a different section of the law, neither limited the meaning of "call" to that section, and the same word is presumed to mean the same thing throughout a statute.

On the phone-number argument, the court found the complaint detailed enough. It listed the approximate dates of the texts, their marketing purpose, and when the number was put on the Do Not Call list.

The company had also asked, for the first time in its reply brief, to pause the case and send the question to the appeals court right away. The court denied that request, finding no "substantial ground for difference of opinion" within the Ninth Circuit. It said that the Ninth Circuit's consistent view differing from the Seventh Circuit's is not enough to justify an immediate appeal.

What it could mean in the Eastern District of California

This ruling moved the Eastern District of California from grey to green on our map. Before it, we had not found a qualifying ruling on this question in this district.

The ruling does not mean the company broke the law. The case continues, and the plaintiff still has to prove his claims. It is also one ruling by one judge, and other judges in the district are not bound by it. But it relies on Ninth Circuit precedent that every federal court in California must follow, so it lines up with how other courts in the Ninth Circuit have ruled.

The bigger picture

On July 14, 2026, the Seventh Circuit held in Steidinger v. Blackstone Medical Services that § 227(c)(5) does not let people sue over texts. It is the only federal appeals court ruling on this exact question, and it binds the federal courts in Illinois, Indiana and Wisconsin.

Since then:

  • Several district courts outside the Ninth Circuit have followed Steidinger, including courts in Colorado, northern Alabama, western Virginia, western Missouri, the middle district of North Carolina, and the middle and southern districts of Florida.
  • Every district court in the Ninth Circuit that has ruled on it has declined to follow Steidinger: the Central District of California (Miller; Combs), the Western District of Washington (Hopkins), this court, and the Northern District of California (Arguello, decided the next day).
  • Some districts are split by judge. In the Southern District of Florida, one judge ruled in September 2026 that texts are not calls (Phillips v. GJD Stars), while another judge there ruled in November 2025 that they are.

Part of the reason this is now contested is the Supreme Court's 2025 decision in McLaughlin Chiropractic Associates v. McKesson Corp., which said courts in private lawsuits are not bound by the Federal Communications Commission's reading of the TCPA. The FCC had long treated texts as calls.

Eastern Pennsylvania federal judge says Do Not Call law covers marketing texts (Dovberg v. Haller Enterprises)

Not legal advice. This is a plain-language summary of court orders and opinions we found, for general information only. It is not legal advice and does not create an attorney-client relationship. If you have a specific situation, talk to a licensed attorney.

On October 21, 2026, a federal judge in the Eastern District of Pennsylvania refused to end a Do Not Call lawsuit over two referral-offer texts, ruling after his own analysis of the law that texts count as "telephone calls" under 47 U.S.C. § 227(c)(5).

CaseDovberg v. Haller Enterprises, Inc.
CourtEastern District of Pennsylvania (E.D. Pa., 3rd Circuit)
Docket / citationNo. 5:26-cv-04286, ECF No. 20 (opinion)
JudgeJoseph F. Leeson, Jr., U.S. District Judge
DecidedOctober 21, 2026
Type of rulingRuling on a contested motion for judgment on the pleadings (denied)
HoldingSection 227(c)(5) covers text messages. A cell phone used for personal purposes can be a "residential" line for Do Not Call purposes, and § 227(c)(5) also allows claims under the FCC's caller-ID rule for telemarketers.
This district on the map todayText treated as a call See all 94 districts on the map

What happened in this case

Marvin Dovberg says he received two unsolicited text messages from Haller Enterprises, Inc. on June 4, 2026. According to the complaint, the texts asked him to refer friends and family and offered "up to $250" when a referred job was complete. He says his cell phone number had been on the National Do Not Call Registry for at least 31 days, that he uses the phone for personal and household purposes, and that he had not used Haller's services since the summer of 2022.

He also says the caller ID on the texts showed "MIDDLETOWN, PA" rather than the company's name, so he could not tell from the caller ID who sent them.

He filed a proposed class action on June 22, 2026, with two claims: one under the Do Not Call rules and one under the federal caller-ID rule for telemarketers. No class has been approved.

Key terms

  • Do Not Call list (National Do Not Call Registry): a free federal list where people can register their phone number to opt out of most telemarketing.
  • 47 U.S.C. § 227(c)(5): the part of the Telephone Consumer Protection Act (TCPA) that lets a person who received "more than one telephone call within any 12-month period" from the same company, in violation of the telemarketing rules, bring their own lawsuit. The law allows up to $500 per violation, or up to three times that if the violation was willful.
  • Motion for judgment on the pleadings: like a motion to dismiss, but filed after the defendant has answered the complaint. It asks the judge to rule for the defendant based only on the written filings, without any evidence. The judge views the facts in the light most favorable to the other side.

What the court decided

Haller asked Judge Joseph F. Leeson, Jr. to rule in its favor on three main grounds. The judge rejected all of them and denied the motion.

1. Texts are "calls." Haller argued that § 227(c)(5) covers only telephone calls, not texts. The judge disagreed after doing his own analysis of the law. He relied on:

  • The ordinary meaning of "call," including a 1991 dictionary definition: "to get or try to get into communication by telephone." Texting is one way of doing that.
  • The law's definition of a "telephone solicitation," which covers a "telephone call or message."
  • The Federal Communications Commission's long-standing view that texts are covered, including a 2024 rule applying Do Not Call protections to texts. After the Supreme Court's 2025 McLaughlin decision, courts are not bound by the FCC's view, but the judge found it persuasive.
  • A 2016 Supreme Court case, Campbell-Ewald Co. v. Gomez, which said it was "undisputed" that a text qualifies as a call under a related section of the law.

He also noted that three other judges in the same district had reached the same conclusion after their own analysis.

2. Cell phones can be "residential." The Do Not Call rules protect "residential" telephone subscribers. The judge held that a person who uses a cell phone for personal purposes can qualify.

3. Caller-ID claims. The judge held that § 227(c)(5) also lets people bring claims under the FCC's rule requiring telemarketers to send proper caller-ID information. He found the complaint stated such a claim even though the texts named Haller in their body, because the caller ID showed only a location.

What it could mean in the Eastern District of Pennsylvania

The Eastern District of Pennsylvania is green on our map. By our count, every judge in this district who has ruled on the question (Judges Henry, Perez, Murphy, Savage and now Leeson) has held that texts are calls under § 227(c)(5).

This ruling does not mean Haller broke the law. It came at an early stage, the facts have not been tested, and the case continues. Other judges are not bound by it, and it could be revisited on appeal later. But it adds to a consistent pattern in this district.

The ruling is already being cited elsewhere. Plaintiffs in pending cases in the Middle District of Pennsylvania (Bronstin v. Jane Technologies) and the Eastern District of Michigan (Soboleski v. Crumble) filed it with those courts as new supporting authority on October 21 and 22, 2026. Those courts have not ruled yet.

The bigger picture

The Third Circuit, the federal appeals court over Pennsylvania, New Jersey, Delaware and the Virgin Islands, has not decided this question for § 227(c)(5). Federal courts in New Jersey have also treated texts as calls (Owen-Brooks; Rubin v. Staples).

Nationally, the landscape changed on July 14, 2026, when the Seventh Circuit held in Steidinger v. Blackstone Medical Services that § 227(c)(5) does not let people sue over texts. It binds the federal courts in Illinois, Indiana and Wisconsin and is the only appeals court ruling on this exact question. (Judge Leeson's opinion does not discuss Steidinger.)

Since then:

  • Several district courts have followed Steidinger, including courts in Colorado, northern Alabama, western Virginia, western Missouri, the middle district of North Carolina, and the middle and southern districts of Florida.
  • Every district court in the Ninth Circuit (the West Coast and nearby states) that has ruled on it has declined to follow Steidinger, most recently in the Eastern District of California (Lewis, October 21) and the Northern District of California (Arguello, October 22).
  • Some districts are split by judge. In the Southern District of Florida, one judge ruled in September 2026 that texts are not calls (Phillips v. GJD Stars), while another judge there ruled in November 2025 that they are.

Oklahoma federal judge rules marketing texts are not "calls" under the Do Not Call law (Reaves v. Worldwide Golf Shops)

On September 14, 2026, a federal judge in the Western District of Oklahoma dismissed a proposed class action over marketing texts sent to a number on the National Do Not Call Registry, ruling that the part of the federal telemarketing law that lets consumers bring their own lawsuits covers telephone calls, not text messages. It is the first ruling on this question we have found from that district.

CaseReaves v. Worldwide Golf Shops, LLC
CourtWestern District of Oklahoma (W.D. Okla., 10th Circuit)
Docket / citationNo. 5:25-cv-00453-JD, ECF No. 40
JudgeJodi W. Dishman, U.S. District Judge
DecidedSeptember 14, 2026
Type of rulingRuling on a contested motion to dismiss (granted, without prejudice)
HoldingText messages are not "telephone calls" under 47 U.S.C. § 227(c)(5), so a Do Not Call claim based only on texts was dismissed. The court followed the Seventh Circuit's Steidinger decision.
This district on the map todayText not treated as a call See all 94 districts on the map

What happened in this case

Paul Reaves says his phone number has been on the National Do Not Call Registry since 2017. According to his complaint, the number belongs to a cell phone on a personal plan that he uses for personal and household purposes, much as someone would use a home landline.

He says that between December 2023 and April 2024 he received at least three telemarketing text messages from Worldwide Golf Shops, LLC, and that he never asked for or agreed to them. He sued under the Telephone Consumer Protection Act (TCPA), a 1991 federal law about telemarketing, and filed the case as a proposed class action on behalf of other people on the Registry who received more than one marketing text from the company within a year. These are allegations; the court did not decide whether they are true.

Key terms

  • Do Not Call list (National Do Not Call Registry): a free federal list where people can register their phone number to opt out of most telemarketing.
  • 47 U.S.C. § 227(c)(5): the part of the TCPA that lets a person who received "more than one telephone call within any 12-month period" from the same company, in violation of the Do Not Call rules, bring their own lawsuit.
  • Motion to dismiss: an early request asking the judge to end a case because, even if everything the plaintiff says is true, the law does not give them a claim. The judge does not decide who is telling the truth at this stage.
  • Dismissed without prejudice: the case is ended for now, but the court did not rule out a new or corrected filing.

What the court decided

Worldwide Golf Shops asked Judge Jodi W. Dishman to dismiss the case. It raised several arguments, but the judge found one of them decided the case: § 227(c)(5) gives a right to sue over "telephone calls," and the complaint described only text messages.

Mr. Reaves argued that texts count as calls. Judge Dishman disagreed. Her reasons, in plain terms:

  • The words of the law. The TCPA does not define "telephone call," so the court looked at dictionaries from 1991, when the law was passed, as the Seventh Circuit did in Steidinger v. Blackstone Medical Services. Quoting that decision, she wrote that "in 1991, a 'telephone call' referred to communication via sound."
  • A different section of the law. Cases that treat texts as calls, such as the Ninth Circuit's Satterfield v. Simon & Schuster, interpreted a different section, Section 227(b), which covers autodialed and prerecorded calls. The judge noted that Section 227(b) and Section 227(c) use different language.
  • The FCC's view. The Federal Communications Commission has long treated texts as calls. The judge explained that after the Supreme Court's 2025 decision in McLaughlin Chiropractic Associates v. McKesson Corp., courts are not bound by the agency's reading and must interpret the statute themselves. She added that the FCC statement cited by Mr. Reaves interpreted Section 227(b), not Section 227(c).

She concluded: "§ 227(c)(5) does not apply to text messages." Because the complaint did not allege any phone calls, it failed to state a claim. Because his own claim failed, Mr. Reaves also could not pursue the claims of the proposed class. The judge noted that the Tenth Circuit, the appeals court for Oklahoma, "has yet to address the issue."

What happened next

The court entered a judgment dismissing the case without prejudice the same day. Three days later the court struck an amended complaint Mr. Reaves filed, because it came after the judgment. On September 22, 2026, he asked the court to alter the judgment and allow an amended complaint. That request was fully briefed by October 19, 2026, and the docket we reviewed showed no ruling on it yet.

What it could mean in the Western District of Oklahoma

The Western District of Oklahoma is red on the map on tcpamap.com (text not treated as a call). That color rests on this order, which is the only order from the district that we found deciding the question. We count it as a contested decision that texts are not calls under § 227(c)(5).

The ruling is not a finding that anyone did or did not break the law. It decides only the motion in this case. One trial judge's ruling also does not bind other judges in the district, and the decision could still change if the court grants the pending request or if the question reaches the Tenth Circuit. Text-message claims under other parts of the TCPA, such as the autodialer section, were not decided here.

The bigger picture

On July 14, 2026, the Seventh Circuit decided Steidinger, holding that § 227(c)(5) does not let people sue over texts. It is the only federal appeals court decision on this exact question, and it binds the federal courts in Illinois, Indiana and Wisconsin.

Since then, many district courts outside the Ninth Circuit have followed it. Within the Tenth Circuit, two judges in the District of Colorado reached the same result in September 2026 (Radvansky v. 1st Class Medical and Brown v. Hursh Group), and this Oklahoma ruling points the same way. Courts in northern Alabama, western Virginia, western Missouri, the middle district of North Carolina and the middle district of Florida have also followed Steidinger.

Other courts disagree. Every district court in the Ninth Circuit that has ruled on the question has declined to follow Steidinger, relying on Ninth Circuit cases that treat texts as calls. The Eastern District of Pennsylvania has also held that texts are calls. And some districts are split by judge: in the Southern District of Florida, one judge ruled in September 2026 that texts are not calls (Phillips v. GJD Stars), while another ruled in November 2025 that they are (the Office Depot cases).
