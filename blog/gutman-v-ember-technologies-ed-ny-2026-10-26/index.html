Brooklyn federal judge rules unwanted marketing texts are covered by the Do Not Call lawsuit provision (Gutman v. Ember Technologies)

In a decision dated October 2026 and entered on the docket October 26, 2026, a federal judge in Brooklyn refused to dismiss a proposed class action over marketing texts for Ember products, ruling that texts are covered by the part of the federal telemarketing law that lets consumers bring their own Do Not Call lawsuits.

CaseGutman v. Ember Technologies, Inc. and gr0.com LLC
CourtEastern District of New York (E.D.N.Y., 2nd Circuit)
Docket / citationNo. 1:25-cv-02436-NGG-PCG, ECF No. 54
JudgeNicholas G. Garaufis, U.S. District Judge
DecidedOctober 26, 2026
Type of rulingRuling on contested motions to dismiss (denied)
HoldingText messages are covered by 47 U.S.C. § 227(c)(5), so repeated marketing texts to a number on the Do Not Call list, or after a "Stop" request, can support a lawsuit. The court reached that reading independently of the FCC and rejected decisions following the Seventh Circuit's view.

What happened in this case

Michael Gutman says he registered his phone number on the National Do Not Call Registry in 2021. He says he then received a series of telemarketing text messages for Ember products in 2024 and 2025, including texts in April 2025 after he had replied "Stop" in February 2024.

He sued Ember Technologies and gr0.com LLC, a marketing company he says sent the texts with Ember's approval, under the Telephone Consumer Protection Act (TCPA), a 1991 federal law about telemarketing. He filed it as a proposed class action, meaning he asks to represent others who got similar texts. These are his allegations; the court has not decided whether they are true.

Key terms

  • Do Not Call list (National Do Not Call Registry): a free federal list where people can register their phone number to opt out of most telemarketing.
  • 47 U.S.C. § 227(c)(5): the part of the TCPA that lets a person who received "more than one telephone call within any 12-month period" from the same company, in violation of the telemarketing rules, bring their own lawsuit.
  • Motion to dismiss: a request asking the judge to end a case early because, even if everything the plaintiff says is true, the law does not give them a claim.

What the court decided

Both companies argued that texts are not covered by § 227(c)(5). Judge Nicholas G. Garaufis denied their motions. His main reasons, in plain terms:

  • After the Supreme Court's Loper Bright and McLaughlin decisions, he did his own reading of the statute rather than relying on the Federal Communications Commission. He acknowledged that texting did not exist when the law passed, but said general terms like "call" can cover later technology, writing that "the language used is more than broad enough to cover the Texts."
  • The same words appear in the neighboring section of the law, § 227(b), and the Second Circuit, the appeals court over New York, has treated that section as covering texts. He found that persuasive, since identical words in the same statute are generally read the same way.
  • He was not persuaded by the argument that "call" must mean something different because the law uses the word "message" elsewhere.
  • He rejected decisions that went the other way, including the Illinois decision that the Seventh Circuit affirmed in Steidinger and the Florida decision in Davis v. CVS Pharmacy, calling them in the minority and unpersuasive.

The case now goes to discovery, the stage where each side gathers evidence.

What it could mean in the Eastern District of New York

The Eastern District of New York is green on the map on tcpamap.com (text treated as a call). That color rests on this order, which is the first contested decision we have found there, and it treats texts as calls.

It is not a finding that either company did anything wrong; the plaintiff still has to prove his allegations. One trial judge's ruling also does not bind other judges in the district, and the Second Circuit has not decided this exact question.

The bigger picture

On July 14, 2026, the Seventh Circuit decided Steidinger v. Blackstone Medical Services, holding that § 227(c)(5) does not let people sue over texts. It is the only federal appeals court decision on this exact question so far, and it binds the federal courts in Illinois, Indiana and Wisconsin.

Since then, district courts have split. Several outside the Seventh Circuit have followed Steidinger, while courts in the Ninth Circuit and in Texas have declined to. Some districts are split by judge: in the Southern District of Florida, different judges have gone opposite ways.

The question opened up partly because of the Supreme Court's 2025 decision in McLaughlin Chiropractic Associates v. McKesson Corp., which said courts in private lawsuits do not have to follow the Federal Communications Commission's reading of the TCPA. Courts now decide the meaning themselves.

When this post was written (October 31, 2026), the map on tcpamap.com showed 18 districts where a text was not treated as a call (red), 18 where it was (green), 1 where judges disagree (yellow) and 57 with no verified decision identified (grey), out of 94.
