Text not treated as a callN.D. Tex.Decided November 16, 2026Posted November 18, 2026On the blog page

Dallas federal judge rules marketing texts are not "calls" under the Do Not Call lawsuit provision (Starling v. What If Holdings)

On November 16, 2026, a federal judge in Dallas dismissed a proposed class action over at least 24 marketing texts, ruling that texts are not "telephone calls" under the part of the federal telemarketing law that lets consumers bring their own Do Not Call lawsuits. It is the second Texas federal district judge we have found to reach that result, after a Southern District of Texas ruling earlier this month, and it goes the other way from an earlier ruling by the same judge.

CaseStarling v. What If Holdings LLC d/b/a C4R Media Corp.
CourtNorthern District of Texas (N.D. Tex., 5th Circuit)
Docket / citationNo. 3:26-cv-01039-B, ECF No. 24
JudgeJane J. Boyle, U.S. District Judge
DecidedNovember 16, 2026
Type of rulingRuling on a contested motion to dismiss (granted; dismissed without prejudice, with leave to amend by December 1, 2026)
HoldingText messages are not "telephone calls" under 47 U.S.C. § 227(c)(5), so a Do Not Call claim based only on texts fails. The court also dismissed a related caller-ID claim because the plaintiff alleged she received only texts.

What happened in this case

Ryleigh Starling sued What If Holdings LLC, which does business as C4R Media Corp., as a proposed class action, meaning she asked to represent other people in a similar situation. She says the company sent her at least twenty-four unwanted telemarketing text messages while her number was on the National Do Not Call Registry. She does not say she received any phone calls or other communications from the company. These are allegations; the court did not decide whether they are true.

She brought two claims under the Telephone Consumer Protection Act (TCPA), a 1991 federal law about telemarketing. The first was a Do Not Call claim. The second said the company did not send the caller identification information that a Federal Communications Commission (FCC) rule requires telemarketers to transmit. The case was filed in federal court in Oregon in February 2026 and moved to the Northern District of Texas in April 2026 by agreement of the parties. The company then asked the court to dismiss it.

Key terms

  • Do Not Call list (National Do Not Call Registry): a free federal list where people can register their phone number to opt out of most telemarketing.
  • 47 U.S.C. § 227(c)(5): the part of the TCPA that lets a person who received "more than one telephone call within any 12-month period" from the same company, in violation of the telemarketing rules, bring their own lawsuit.
  • Motion to dismiss: a request asking the judge to end a case early because, even if everything the plaintiff says is true, the law does not give her a claim.
  • Dismissed without prejudice: the case is dismissed for now, but the plaintiff is not permanently barred from refiling or amending.

What the court decided

Judge Jane J. Boyle granted the motion. She wrote that "neither the ordinary meaning of 'telephone call' nor statutory context support including text messages as telephone calls in § 227(c)(5)." Her main reasons, in plain terms:

  • Ordinary meaning. She looked at 1991 dictionaries, which defined a telephone by reference to sound. She wrote that "whether in 1991 or today, the ordinary public usage of the noun 'telephone call' is not synonymous with nor does it encompass 'text message.'" She added: "A person asking for a 'telephone call' will not expect to receive a written message, or text."
  • The rest of the statute. In 2018 and 2019, Congress amended other parts of the TCPA to mention text messages expressly, but did not add similar language to § 227(c)(5). Other parts of § 227(c) also use the broader term "telephone solicitation." The judge said she could not conclude that Congress meant the lawsuit provision to reach people who received texts.
  • The FCC's view. The FCC has long treated texts as calls. Relying on the Supreme Court's 2024 Loper Bright decision, the judge said courts must decide the meaning of the statute themselves. She found the FCC guidance "unpersuasive" and declined to follow it.
  • Cases going the other way. She acknowledged "a growing split between district courts" and discussed Texas rulings that treated texts as calls, including Duron v. Kings Capital Holding (Western District of Texas). She said many of those decisions did not do their own analysis of the statute's words, and she declined to follow them.

The caller-ID claim failed too. The judge said it did not matter which part of the TCPA the FCC's caller-ID rule came from: if it came from a part with no private lawsuit right, there is no claim, and if it came from § 227(c), the claim fails because Starling received only texts, not telephone calls.

The dismissal is without prejudice. The judge gave Starling until December 1, 2026 to amend her complaint "to allege facts beyond unwanted receipt of text messages." In a footnote, she said amending to restate a § 227(c)(5) claim based on texts alone would be futile under this opinion.

What it could mean in the Northern District of Texas

With this order, the map on tcpamap.com shows the Northern District of Texas in red (text not treated as a call). The district had been shown green based on Callier v. The Finish Line (September 2026), where Judge Boyle adopted a magistrate judge's recommendation that the Do Not Call provision covers texts. Because this new ruling comes from the same judge and is the most recent contested ruling in the district, the map follows it rather than showing the district as split. The new opinion does not mention Callier v. The Finish Line.

This is not a finding about whether anyone did anything wrong; it is a ruling about what the federal statute allows people to sue over. One trial judge's ruling does not bind other judges in the district, and other Northern District of Texas judges have motions on this question in front of them. The Fifth Circuit, which covers Texas, Louisiana and Mississippi, has not decided the question, and the ruling could be reviewed on appeal. The plaintiff may also amend her complaint by December 1.

The bigger picture

On July 14, 2026, the Seventh Circuit decided Steidinger v. Blackstone Medical Services, holding that § 227(c)(5) does not let people sue over texts. It is the only federal appeals court decision on this exact question so far, and it binds the federal courts in Illinois, Indiana and Wisconsin. Judge Boyle cited it, along with several district court rulings reaching the same result, and wrote that the court "is by no means alone in the opinion it holds today."

Texas federal courts are now divided across districts. On November 6, 2026, Judge Drew B. Tipton in the Southern District of Texas held in Siringi v. Crust Pizza Co. that texts are not calls, and an earlier Southern District of Texas ruling had gone the other way, so that district is split. On the same day as this order, the Western District of Texas again ruled that texts are calls, in Waterman v. The General Automobile Insurance Services. Courts in the Ninth Circuit have declined to follow Steidinger.

The question opened up partly because of the Supreme Court's 2025 decision in McLaughlin Chiropractic Associates v. McKesson Corp., which said courts in private lawsuits do not have to follow the FCC's reading of the TCPA. Courts now decide the meaning themselves.

As of November 18, 2026, with this order included, the map on tcpamap.com shows 19 districts where a text was not treated as a call (red), 16 where it was (green), 2 where judges disagree (yellow) and 57 with no verified decision identified (grey), out of 94.
